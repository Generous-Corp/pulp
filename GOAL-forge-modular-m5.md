## The goal, in one paragraph

On the M5, open Forge Modular — as the standalone app, and as an AU, VST3 and
CLAP plugin inside a DAW — type a prompt, and get a working VCV Rack **module**.
Switch to Patch, type a prompt, and get a Rack **patch** that opens, makes sound,
explains itself at three depths, and **is recognisably the kind of patch that was
asked for** — because it was built against a library of real idioms and checked
against them, not invented. The machine is set up by running one script.

## Part 2 — The patches are worth distributing

This is the part that does not exist yet, and the reason the goal was rewritten.

Today a prompt produces a plausible module graph, not a good patch. "An evolving
ambient drone" returns oscillators into a filter into a delay — a shape the model
has read about. Listening to it settles the question: it is a tone, not a piece.
A krell patch played a single note. A bouncing-ball rhythm had no trigger source
at all. Every one passed every gate we have, because the gates check that a patch
*sounds*, never that it is *the thing that was asked for*.

A module never had this problem: `dsp_vocabulary.py` extracts what a module may
be built from and injects it into the module contract. Patches have no
equivalent. Build one.

### 2a — A patch vocabulary, built the way the DSP vocabulary is

```
tools/rack/patch_idioms/*.json     the library — one file per family
tools/rack/patch_vocabulary.py     renders it into the patch contract
tools/rack/idiom_check.py          asserts a patch against its claimed idiom
```

Same mechanism as the DSP side, including the install-time check that the
rendered vocabulary is non-empty — an empty one is exactly how the DSP side
failed silently, and it cost three model calls per run to discover.

Each idiom carries what it *is*, its **required topology**, its expected
**behaviour**, what "sounds right" means for it, and the mistakes that make it
fail. One record does four jobs: teaches the model, checks the result, drives the
behavioural gate, and explains a rejection in words a person recognises.

**Scale: roughly sixty idioms across families** — voice, generative, rhythm,
modulation, texture, utility. Not a handful. Coverage is what separates a demo
from a system.

### 2b — Verify behaviour, not just audibility

The idiom names the STRUCTURE; this measures the BEHAVIOUR. Both, or neither is
worth much — structure without behaviour is the krell that wired its loop
correctly and played one note; behaviour without structure would pass a drone off
as a rhythm because something moved.

Measured on the audio the patch gate already renders: does it keep going (onsets
over ~10 s), does it move (amplitude and spectral variance), is it rhythmic when
asked (interval clustering; lengthening intervals for "slows as it settles"), is
it in range.

Topology first, because it is instant and its failures are specific; audio
second, because rendering costs seconds.

### The bar for "worth distributing"

- A prompt naming or implying an idiom is checked against its topology **before**
  the audio gate, and a rejection names the missing connection rather than
  saying "no sound".
- At least **a dozen prompts across different families** each produce a patch
  that passes its idiom check and its behavioural assertions — **proven by
  running them**, not by reading the library.
- The three failures already found are regression cases: a krell that plays one
  note fails, a bouncing ball with no trigger source fails, a drone that never
  evolves fails.

## Where the content comes from

The body of knowledge these instruments actually have. Allen Strange's
*Electronic Music: Systems, Techniques and Controls* for the classical
voltage-control repertoire; Bjørn & Meyer's *Patch & Tweak* for modern Eurorack
practice; the manufacturer manuals that document their own idioms best — Make
Noise Maths and its function-generator patches, Mutable's design notes, Buchla's
LPG-centred west-coast approach; Rob Hordijk's Rungler and the chaos lineage;
Todd Barton's Krell work.

**Techniques, not text.** A Krell patch IS "a random voltage sampled to set an
envelope's decay, with end-of-cycle retriggering it." That is a method, and
describing it in our own words is fair use of an idea. Copying a book's prose or
ingesting a community patch library is neither necessary nor permitted here.

**Explicitly not in scope:** deciding whether a patch is *good*. Taste is not
measurable, and a gate claiming to measure it would be lying. What is measurable
is whether a patch is the KIND of thing it claims to be and behaves the way that
kind behaves. That is a floor, not a ceiling — but the floor is missing today,
which is why a generic tone passes as an evolving drone.

## How to spend the time

The CLI is the cheap loop and everything about generation quality is debuggable
there in one command. The app and REAPER prove wiring — necessary, but slow and
focus-sensitive. Use the cheap loop to find defects and the expensive ones to
confirm the paths.

Two patterns have accounted for nearly every real bug so far, and both will
recur:

**One list, two writers.** A manifest naming 29 modules while a header declared
24 and a source registered 25; two generators wording success differently; a
gate reading defaults while Rack loaded stored values; two `tools_dir()`
implementations; two things registering the same model, which crashed Rack at
launch. When something is described in more than one place, derive it in one.

**The claim was never the thing asserted.** A green suite beside a screen saying
"Built" and "file is not there" at once. A crop box positioned above the text it
was watching. "Build did not reach the generator" when Build was never pressed.
When a test passes, ask what would have to break for it to fail — and break it.
